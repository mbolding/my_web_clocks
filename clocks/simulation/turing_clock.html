Turing pattern clock

after Alan Turing, “The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis”, 1952

In 1952 Alan Turing asked how a ball of identical cells could ever become a striped or spotted animal. His answer was two chemicals, each made and used up by reactions in the cells, and each spreading out by diffusion, one faster than the other. Left alone, the mixture does not stay even: small random differences grow into a steady pattern of spots or stripes, with a spacing set by the chemistry, not by any plan.

This clock runs a later model of the same kind, from Peter Gray and Stephen Scott in the 1980s, which is easy to compute and makes clean Turing patterns. Chemical U is fed in everywhere; chemical V turns U into more of itself and is slowly removed. Every cell of a 400 by 400 grid follows the same two rules, many times a second:

U += 1.0·∇²U − U·V² + F·(1 − U) V += 0.5·∇²V + U·V² − (F + k)·V

Only the two numbers F and k differ from place to place. Inside the shapes of the hands and the hour marks they are set where patterns can live; everywhere else V dies away. Nothing draws the hands. As they turn, the pattern grows into the new ground ahead of them and fades from the ground behind, organising itself afresh every moment. The second dot runs with spots, and leaves a short trail of them as it goes.

Choose stripes, spots or a maze to change the chemistry inside the hands, and switch to “where patterns can live” to see the map of F and k that the time draws.
