Neural oscillator clock

a pacemaker of two neurons, three rings that count, and a light to keep them honest

In the middle are two neurons, A and B, that inhibit each other. Whichever is firing holds the other quiet, but firing tires it, and as it tires the other escapes and takes over. So they take turns, without any clock telling them to. Thomas Graham Brown proposed this half-centre circuit in 1911 for the rhythm of walking; this one uses Kiyotoshi Matsuoka's 1985 model of it, tuned to alternate about once a second.

τ·xᵢ′ = −xᵢ − b·vᵢ − w·yⱼ + s yᵢ = max(0, xᵢ) 2τ·vᵢ′ = −vᵢ + yᵢ (v is the tiredness)

Each time A starts a burst, it sends a beat to the outer ring of sixty neurons. Only one of them is active at a time, and each beat hands the activity on to its neighbour, like a bump moving round a ring attractor of the kind used to model head-direction cells. When the bump passes from 59 back to 0, a carry neuron passes a beat to the minute ring, and the minute ring does the same for the twelve neurons of the hour ring. The bright neuron in each ring is a hand.

Light

Left to itself, this pacemaker runs a little slow, with a natural period of 1.05 seconds. A body clock is the same: kept in the dark, the human circadian rhythm runs a little longer than 24 hours, and it is daylight, reaching the brain's suprachiasmatic nucleus, that pulls it back into step each day. Here a short pulse of light excites B at the start of every real second. It can't make the oscillator faster or slower, only nudge its phase, but a nudge every cycle is enough to lock it: the network then counts exactly one beat per second, a steady fraction of a second behind the light.

Turn the light off and the network free-runs: it keeps perfect order, but every beat comes a little late, and the clock falls behind, about three seconds a minute at 1.05. Turn it back on and the rhythm locks again within a few cycles, but the seconds it lost stay lost, the way jet lag fixes the rhythm before it fixes the hour. “Set from the clock” resets the rings. The light holds natural periods from about 0.8 to 1.35 seconds. Slower than that, it can't pull every cycle into a second and beats slip; faster, two beats fit into each second, and the network locks just as firmly, counting double.
