Tower of Hanoi clock

seventeen discs, one move a second since midnight

The Tower of Hanoi is a puzzle published by the French mathematician Édouard Lucas in 1883. A tower of discs, smallest on top, must move to another peg, one disc at a time, and a disc may never sit on a smaller one. To move n discs you have to move the n − 1 above the largest out of the way, move the largest, then stack the n − 1 back on top of it. So the shortest solution takes 2n − 1 moves, and there is only one way to do it in that many.

This clock plays that shortest solution for seventeen discs, from peg A towards peg C, at one move a second counted from midnight. The tower is a picture of the number of seconds since midnight: at 09:15:30 it has made 9 × 3600 + 15 × 60 + 30 = 33,330 moves.

A binary counter in disguise

Write the move number k in binary. The disc that moves is one more than the number of zeros at the end of k: every odd move is disc 1, moves 2, 6, 10 and so on are disc 2, and disc d moves every 2d moves, first at move 2d−1. Each disc also always travels round the pegs the same way. With seventeen discs the odd-numbered ones step A → C → B → A and the even ones A → B → C → A, which is what the two colours show. Put together, after k moves:

moves of disc d = ⌊(k + 2d−1) / 2d⌋
its peg = that many steps round its cycle, mod 3

That is how this page draws the tower: straight from the move number, without replaying the day. Bit d − 1 of the Gray code is 1 exactly when disc d has moved an odd number of times, so each move flips one Gray bit, the moved disc's own.

Why seventeen

A day has 86,400 seconds. Sixteen discs would need 216 − 1 = 65,535 moves and be finished at 18:12:15. Seventeen need 131,071, so the tower is never finished: the day ends at move 86,399, about 66% of the way, and at midnight the discs start again on peg A. At 18:12:15 the sixteen smaller discs stand together on peg B, and at 18:12:16 the largest disc makes its only move of the day, from A to C.
