Factor clock

the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, once a second

Every whole number bigger than 1 can be written as a product of primes, and in only one way, apart from the order of the factors. Primes are the numbers that can't be split, like 2, 3, 5, 7 and 11. Euclid had the key step, and Gauss stated and proved the whole theorem in 1801. It is why primes are called the building blocks of the whole numbers: each number has exactly one recipe.

This clock counts the seconds since midnight, from 0 to 86,399, and each second factorises the count and draws it. At noon the count is 43,200, which is 2⁶ × 3³ × 5²; a whole day is 86,400 seconds, 2⁷ × 3³ × 5².

How to read a diagram

A diagram of n = p × m is p copies of the diagram of m, set evenly round a circle; then each copy splits the same way, down to single dots. So there are exactly n dots, and the primes show up as rings inside rings. These pictures were made popular by Brent Yorgey in 2012.

Read from the outside in, and count the groups at each layer: the line under the sum lists them. Two copies sit side by side, and a pair of 2s is drawn as a square of four, so powers of 2 become grids. The smallest factors are on the outside and the largest is innermost. That order keeps the most layers big enough to see: a large prime drawn on the outside would shrink everything inside it to specks, but drawn innermost it is just a ring at the bottom of each copy. Press “show the groups” to outline every copy, coloured by the prime that made it.

A prime has nothing to split, so its diagram is a single ring of dots. When there are too many to draw one by one, the dots run together into a fine unbroken ring with the number written inside. Deep inside a big number the copies get smaller than a pixel too, so a group that small is drawn as one soft disc, as bright as its dots would be.

Two seconds are special. At midnight the count is 0, which is not a product of primes at all: every number divides it, so there is nothing to draw. One second later it is 1, the product of no primes, drawn as a single dot.

Primes in a day

8,403 of the day's 86,400 seconds are prime. They thin out as the day goes on, as the prime number theorem says they should: 17 of the first minute's 60 seconds are prime, but only 9 of the last minute's, and the first hour has 503 against the last hour's 310. The longest wait between two primes is 72 seconds, from 31,397 to 31,469, that is from 08:43:17 to 08:44:29. Use “look at” to try any number of seconds; the clock keeps time while you do, and “back to now” returns to it.
